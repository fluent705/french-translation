When translation falls short

French doesn't
just mean
French.

The same sentence, translated correctly into French, can still be read three different ways depending on where your reader sits—Paris, Montréal, or Abidjan.

I
When the register is wrong

Legal French in France leans formal and precise. In Québec, statutory language follows its own civil law tradition. Using the wrong register in the wrong jurisdiction doesn't just sound off—it raises questions about the document's authority.

II
When idiom doesn't travel

A phrase that's perfectly natural in English can arrive in French sounding literal, cold, or unintentionally humorous. In a marketing campaign, that's a missed connection. In a medical document, it's a liability.

III
When terminology shifts meaning

A single term—"contrat," "accord," "convention"—carries different legal weight depending on the French-speaking jurisdiction. I don't guess. I research, verify, and translate with the precision your document demands.

The language behind the language

French carries
culture in every word

Every dialect, region, and professional field brings its own French to the table. Knowing the language is the starting point—knowing its world is what makes translation work.

France · Legal
Jurisprudence
Case Law
In French civil law, jurisprudence carries formal weight that "case law" alone doesn't capture in English. Context, jurisdiction, and the document's purpose all shape the right translation choice.
Québec · Real Estate
Promesse d'achat
Offer to Purchase
The Québec real estate system operates under the Civil Code—meaning promesse d'achat is a binding legal instrument with obligations not captured by simply writing "offer."
Clinical · Medical
Consentement éclairé
Informed Consent
Medical transcreation demands that patients understand—truly understand—what they're agreeing to. Éclairé means illuminated, not just informed. The difference matters in a clinical setting.
Marketing · Brand
Savoir-faire
Expertise
Translating "savoir-faire" as "expertise" is technically correct and emotionally flat. Great marketing transcreation keeps the feeling intact—even when the exact words can't cross the border.
